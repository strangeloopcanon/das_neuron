A neuron label is not a circuit explanation

A tiny economic network shows what naming the parts leaves out.

I wanted to try a slightly silly experiment: take an economic model, turn it into a tiny neural network, and see what we understood. Could we name the neurons, inspect every weight, and still miss how the parts worked together?

We built the experiment around a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model, or DSGE. Households choose consumption, work and investment. Productivity changes at random. So does the discomfort of working. Installing capital has a cost. Decisions today affect what the household can do tomorrow.

We solved the nonlinear economic equations numerically, then trained networks to approximate their decision rules. The inputs are capital, productivity and work discomfort. The example below has two layers of 16 hidden neurons and three outputs: consumption, labour and investment. That is 387 adjustable weights and biases. Every one is available to inspect.

The distinction matters: the network approximates the solved decision rules at one calibration. It is not an exact translation of the entire economic model.

The economic equations pose a similar problem. Knowing what capital and consumption mean does not solve the household's choices. Future returns, work effort and installation costs must fit together.

What a neuron label tells us

On 2,048 separate discovery examples, neuron H2.0 has a +0.941 correlation with capital. H2.4 tracks capital too (+0.806), but also productivity (+0.481). These are measured associations. Calling H2.0 the capital neuron would imply more than we have established.

Hold capital fixed near its steady-state reference. Raise productivity and the discomfort of working together. H2.0 still contributes +0.01949 to the network's consumption change. Its capital label does not predict that contribution. Other neurons reinforce or offset it.

For each final-layer neuron, multiply its activation change by its output weight and the output scale. Adding all 16 terms recovers the response to floating-point precision. Labels omit those signed contributions and cancellations.

The same labels, a different computation

Now apply the work-discomfort shock alone, at low and high capital. The five largest consumption contributions share just one neuron. The weights and association labels stay fixed. The activation pattern and contribution ranking change with the state.

Each hidden neuron adds a bias to a weighted sum, then sets a negative result to zero. A change can therefore switch neurons on or off downstream. A useful explanation must account for those switches and the resulting output. Naming what each unit tends to track does not do that.

Jacob Hilton and colleagues' Understanding RL Vision goes further in another setting: it tests feature interpretations through targeted edits to a game-playing network. Our economic example illustrates why descriptions of features need a matching account of their joint effects.

From equations to data to a circuit

The training data come from the economic solution. For each state, we calculate consumption, labour and investment, then train the network on those input-output pairs. We assess its predictions on separate states. The economic equations supply a reference answer for the behaviour we want to explain.

That gives us two ways to examine the same decision. The economic model describes household choices and their constraints. The neural circuit implements an approximation through weighted connections and activation switches. Inspecting either set of components still leaves the task of explaining how they combine.

Six features in two coordinates

We also built an exact, constructed circuit for six consumption-response features from six separate growth economies. Each feature has its own direction in a two-dimensional hidden representation. All six share two coordinates.

The decoder recovers every feature exactly when one sector has the specified unit response. Opposite directions cancel when both sectors change, so the decoder misses both responses. This is designed superposition: more known features than dimensions, with measurable interference. The interactive version exposes the entire circuit.

Knowing the weights lets us calculate each result. Naming the components does not supply that calculation. I want explanations that connect the parts to the decision, predict how they combine, and tell us what changes when we alter them.
